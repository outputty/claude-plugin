# Chapter 3: How-to Guides

## Core Idea

A how-to guide is goal-oriented: directions that guide an already-competent user through a real-world problem or toward a result. It is defined by the user's need and project - never by the tool's operations.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **How-to guides addressed to problems, not machinery**: every how-to guide should answer to a human project - what the human needs to do, with the tools at hand, to obtain the result they need. The prevailing anti-pattern defines guides by operations a tool can perform, which addresses no need the user has.
  - How: name the real-world goal first; let tools appear as incidental bit-players, the means to the user's end. A guide may cut across different tools or parts of a system, joined up by what a person needs to get done.
- **How-to characteristics**: focused on tasks or problems; assume the user knows what they want to achieve; action and only action; no digression, explanation, or teaching.
- **Seek flow**: ground sequences in the patterns of the user's activities and thinking so the guide acquires smooth progress. Watch what you ask the user to hold open in their head, how long before a thought resolves in action, and whether jumps back to earlier concerns are avoidable. At its best, a how-to guide is "the documentation equivalent of a helper who has the tool you were about to reach for, ready to place it in your hand."

## Key Concepts

- **Goal-oriented**: concerned with work - navigating a real-world problem-field - in contrast to a tutorial's concern with study.
- **Conditional imperative**: the characteristic sentence form - "If you want x, do y. To achieve w, do z."
- **Not merely procedural**: real-world problems don't always offer linear solutions; sequences may fork, overlap, and have multiple entry and exit points, and may require the user's judgement.
- **Actions include thinking**: a how-to guide addresses how the user thinks as well as what they do.
- **Scope framing**: "how to calibrate the radar array" is a how-to guide; "how to build a web application" is not - that's a vastly open-ended sphere of skill, not a specific goal.

## Mental Models

- A recipe is the model how-to guide: it defines what will be achieved, addresses a specific question, excludes both teaching and discussion, and is followed even by the professional chef who wrote it - to ensure they do it correctly. But following it still requires basic competence: a recipe is not a substitute for a cooking lesson.
- The list of how-to guides frames the picture of what your product can actually do - a rich list is an encouraging suggestion of its capabilities, and well-written guides that address the right questions are likely your most-read pages.
- A how-to guide is a contract: if you're facing this situation, you can work through it by taking these steps.

## Anti-patterns

- **Tool-motions guidance**: "To deploy the desired database configuration, select the appropriate options and press Deploy." Looks like guidance; is mostly useless information anyone competent in the domain already knows, disconnected from purpose. What the user actually needs: which configuration options align with which real-world needs.
- **Completeness creep**: explaining or providing reference "for completeness" dilutes the guide. If they're important, link to them.
- **Over-narrow guides**: a guide useless for anything except the exact narrow case addressed is rarely valuable; stay open enough that users can adapt the guidance to their needs.
- **End-to-end completeness**: unlike a tutorial, a how-to guide should start and end in some reasonable, meaningful place and require the reader to join it up to their own work.

## Worked Example

Naming, from the chapter's own before/after:

- Good: **How to integrate application performance monitoring** - says exactly what the guide shows.
- Bad: **Integrating application performance monitoring** - maybe it's about how to decide whether you should, not how to do it.
- Very bad: **Application performance monitoring** - maybe it's how, maybe it's whether, maybe it's just an explanation of what it is.

Search engines appreciate good titles as much as humans do.

And the language patterns:

- "This guide shows you how to…" - state the problem or task clearly up front.
- "If you want x, do y. To achieve w, do z." - conditional imperatives.
- "Refer to the x reference guide for a full list of options." - don't pollute a practical guide with every possible related thing.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define every how-to guide by a user's real-world goal, never by a tool's operations.
2. Assume competence: the user knows what they want; give action and only action, and link out to explanation and reference.
3. Order steps by necessity first, then by what sets up the user's environment or thinking for later steps; attend to pace and rhythm.
4. Write titles as "How to <verb>…" statements of exactly what the guide shows.
5. Prefer practical usability over completeness; leave reasonable entry and exit points and adaptability to variant cases.
