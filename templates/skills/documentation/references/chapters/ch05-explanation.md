# Chapter 5: Explanation

## Core Idea

Explanation is understanding-oriented: discursive treatment of a subject that permits reflection, bringing clarity, light, and context. It is the only kind of documentation it might make sense to read in the bath - away from the product, away from the work.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Explanation as reflection**: reflection occurs after something else and depends on it, yet brings something new - shines a new light on the subject. Its perspective is higher and wider than the other three forms: not the user's eye-level view (how-to), not the close-up of the machinery (reference), but a bounded topic, "an area of knowledge".
- **The "about" test**: you should be able to place an implicit (or explicit) _about_ in front of every explanation title - "About user authentication", "About database connection policies". Explanation guides are about a topic in the sense that they are _around_ it.
  - When to use: naming and scoping any explanation page; a title that can't take "about" is probably not explanation.
- **The why-question prompt**: tutorials, how-to guides, and reference get their scope from something well-defined (what the user must learn, the task, the machine itself). Explanation is open-ended, so use a real or imagined _why_ question as the prompt - or deliberately draw lines around a reasonable area and be satisfied with that.

## Key Concepts

- **Understanding-oriented**: deepens and broadens understanding; answers "Can you tell me about…?"
- **Alternative names**: Discussion, Background, Conceptual guides, Topics - the section doesn't need to be called Explanation.
- **Etymology as guide**: explanation is unfolding - revealing what is hidden in the folds; understanding shares roots with holding and grasping (comprehend) - making knowledge safely one's own.
- **The web of understanding**: without explanation, the practitioner's knowledge of their craft is loose, fragmented, and fragile, and their exercise of it is anxious.
- **Not a luxury**: explanation is less urgent than the other three, but no less important; no practitioner can afford to be without an understanding of their craft.

## Mental Models

- Writing explanation is weaving a web: make connections to other things, even outside the immediate topic, if that helps.
- Explanation is discussion: it can consider and weigh contrary opinions; all human creation is invested with opinion, and understanding comes from a perspective - which means other perspectives exist and must be considered.
- Things to discuss: the bigger picture, history, choices and alternatives and possibilities, reasons and justifications - design decisions, historical reasons, technical constraints, implications, specific examples.

## Anti-patterns

- **Scattered explanation**: explanation often goes unrecognised as a category, ending up sprinkled in small parcels through other sections - starving the topic and cluttering the host sections.
- **Absorption**: explanation "tends to absorb other things" - the writer intent on covering a topic feels the urge to include instruction or technical description. These belong elsewhere; letting them creep in interferes with the explanation and removes them from view in their correct place. Keep explanation closely bounded.
- **Treating explanation as dispensable**: seen as lesser because it's distant from the practitioner's active concerns - a mistake.

## Worked Example

The language of explanation, pattern by pattern:

- "The reason for x is because historically, y…" - explain.
- "W is better than z, because…" - offer judgements, even opinions, where appropriate.
- "An x in system y is analogous to a w in system z. However…" - provide context that helps the reader.
- "Some users prefer w (because z). This can be a good approach, but…" - weigh up alternatives.
- "An x interacts with a y as follows:…" - unfold the machinery's internal secrets to help understand why it does what it does.

The book-length model is Harold McGee's _On Food and Cooking_ (1984): it teaches no dishes, contains no recipes (except as historical examples), and isn't reference. It places food and cooking in the context of history, society, science, and technology - explaining why we do what we do in the kitchen. Nobody reads it while cooking; we read it to reflect on cooking. Afterward our understanding is changed: what we learned may or may not apply next time we're at the stove, but it changes how we think about the craft, and that affects practice.

## Key Takeaways

1. Bound every explanation by a topic, named so "about" fits in front of it; use a why-question as the scoping prompt.
2. Make connections, provide context and background, discuss alternatives, and admit opinion and perspective - this is the one form where opinion belongs.
3. Keep it closely bounded: push instruction to guides and description to reference the moment they try to creep in.
4. Write it to be read away from the product - if it only makes sense mid-task, it's probably a how-to guide or reference in disguise.
